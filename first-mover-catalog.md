The most useful distinction is between an original name, a distinctive proposal, a working prototype, and the first demonstrated deployment. These are different achievements. A later announcement does not establish that the underlying work began later.

**Assessment key:** A — narrow combination worth investigating (31); B — precedented ingredients or application design (51); C — too thin or technically unresolved (18); S — speculative capability not established (13). A ranks investigation value, not probability of being first. B does not mean every detail has already been invented. Sources identify component or neighboring precedents unless the note explicitly identifies a direct match. A blank comparison means a dedicated precedent search remains open, not that none exists.

**How to establish a stronger first-mover case**

For each A entry, define the smallest testable claim: inputs, transformation, output, failure behavior and constraints. Compare that claim with dated papers, patents, archived products, open-source history and non-English work. Then distinguish first proposal from first working implementation. Hardware needs measured performance against a baseline; software needs an executable implementation that actually enforces the proposed rule; policy programs need a documented launch and operating results. A unique project title, a proposed file tree, an AI-generated endorsement, a “production ready” badge, or a “patent pending” line was not treated as evidence of those milestones.

Some listed environmental products claim carbon capture, potable water, medical protection or energy output without supporting tests. The catalog records the proposed element, not those performance claims. Likewise, document hashes establish byte integrity, not truth, authorship, legal ownership or semantic correctness.
